# About

Hi, I’m Sheen. I’m a musician and educator based in Japan. My work moves between teaching, keyboard playing, arranging and composition, music production, and, more recently, projects involving music and AI.

What connects these different parts of my work is a long-standing curiosity about how music works—and how people learn to hear, understand, and make it for themselves.

## From classical training to popular music

My relationship with music began when I was three years old, at a small rural primary school where my mother was teaching at the time. There was a pedal organ in one of the classrooms, and the first melody I learned to play on it was a Chinese children’s song called *Little Rabbit, Be Good*. I can still remember what it felt like to touch those keys for the first time.

Years later, I found my way into formal music training, eventually studying at a conservatory and completing a master’s degree in 2017. For a long time, my musical life looked fairly conventional: practising repertoire, performing, studying theory, and teaching piano—including the kind of regular piano lessons many children begin with.

But the more I became involved with popular music, the more I began to see another side of musicianship. I discovered improvisation and jazz, and became fascinated by the idea that good music did not always have to begin with a written score. You could listen, imitate, play by ear, experiment with harmony, and create something that sounded complete and convincing without relying on a fully notated score.

After graduate school, I spent some time teaching keyboards at a contemporary music school in China. Moving from a conservatory environment into the world of popular music brought me into closer contact with singers, arrangers, producers, and working musicians—and, more importantly, with very different ways of learning and making music.

## Learning beyond the score

Once I began exploring improvisation more seriously, it opened the door to many other things. To improvise, I needed to understand harmony more deeply. Harmony led naturally to arranging and composition; arranging led me to think more about instrumentation, texture, and orchestration. My interest gradually expanded from playing music to understanding how music is put together.

Along the way, I continued studying music production and scoring, including coursework through Berklee and the Academy of Art University in San Francisco. These experiences gradually broadened both the kind of music I make and the kind of work I take on—from keyboard playing and teaching to arranging, composition, production, and score preparation.

More recently, as AI has begun to reshape parts of music creation and music technology, some of my work has extended in that direction as well. I’ve been involved in projects where musical knowledge and human judgment are used to help evaluate and improve AI systems—another new way of asking questions about how we understand sound and music.

## How people learn music

All of this gradually changed the way I thought about teaching. Conservatory training had given me an important foundation, but I began to realize that it was only one way of learning music. Many people have no intention of becoming professional musicians in the first place—and even among professionals, not everyone learns primarily through formal lessons, written exercises, and notated repertoire.

Listening, imitation, playing by ear, transcription, experimentation, and improvisation can all be powerful ways of learning. Some excellent musicians develop much of their musicianship through exactly these kinds of experiences. To me, this doesn’t make notation any less important. I see it as a written language for music—a way to record, examine, communicate, and share musical ideas—rather than a set of instructions that always has to be followed note for note.

This is also where my interest in informal music learning comes from. I’m particularly interested in the space between formal musical knowledge and the ways people naturally learn through listening, doing, copying, experimenting, and creating. It is an area I hope to continue exploring through both my teaching and future academic research.

That balance is also central to the way I teach. I want students to be comfortable with basic notation, even if their goal is popular music, improvisation, or learning largely by ear. But I also want them to understand what the notation represents—to hear the harmony behind it, make musical choices, accompany, improvise, arrange, and eventually transcribe what they hear for themselves.

## Beyond music

Music takes up a large part of my life, but my curiosity tends to wander well beyond it. I enjoy food, photography and travel, but I’m just as likely to become absorbed in plants, animals, astronomy, geography, or a language I suddenly decide I want to understand.

Languages have been a particular fascination of mine. Over the years I’ve wandered in and out of Italian, French and Korean, among others, usually without getting very far. More recently I’ve even found myself tempted by Hebrew. What keeps drawing me back is not only the language itself, but the way a new language gives you access to people, cultures, and ways of seeing ordinary things differently.

That curiosity was also part of what eventually brought me to live abroad. I now live in Japan, where Japanese has become one of the main languages of my everyday life and work. Living here has often felt very different from what I was used to—the greater sense of personal distance, the attention to rules, and the quieter boundaries between people. At times it can feel distant, but for someone who is quite happy spending long stretches of time quietly making things on his own, I’ve also found that it suits me surprisingly well.

After all these years, I seem to keep coming back to the same thing that fascinated me when I first touched a keyboard as a child: **being curious about sound, and trying to understand how it works.**

This website is, in a way, an extension of that curiosity.

I’ll be gradually collecting my scores, courses, and other work here, along with things I occasionally feel like writing about—music and learning, experiences from living in different places, or simply something I happened to think through one day.

If any of that sounds interesting to you, feel free to come back from time to time.
